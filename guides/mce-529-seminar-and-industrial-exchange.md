# MCE 529 — Seminar and Industrial Exchange Programme

*2 Units · 500 Level, Harmattan · FUTO Mechatronics*

## Overview

This course has two halves. The **seminar** trains you to research a technical topic, write a convincing report, and defend it before an audience. The **industrial exchange** trains you to observe a real plant, record what you see, and turn it into an engineering report. Both are assessed on communication, evidence and professionalism.

There is no written examination. Marks come from the seminar report, the presentation, the industrial exchange report/logbook, and participation.

**Note on scope:** MCE 529 has no BMAS entry. The content below follows standard Nigerian university seminar and industrial-training practice (SIWES framework, departmental seminar guidelines). Confirm the current marking scheme with the course coordinator.

## Course Content Details

### 1. Seminar topic selection and scope

Choose a topic that is current, technical, and narrow enough to master in one semester.

**Study:** Kumar, "Formulating a research problem" (narrowing a topic); Purdue OWL "Writing in Engineering" for the abstract.
**Exam:** No paper. Deliverable: an approved topic and a 200-word abstract.

- [ ] **Topic criteria** — relevance to Mechatronics, available literature, supervisor interest, scope for analysis (not just description).
- [ ] **Topic types** — technology review, comparative study, application case, design concept, policy/industry analysis.
- [ ] **Scope statement** — one paragraph: what the seminar covers, what it excludes, and the time period of the literature.
- [ ] **Title** — specific and informative; avoid "A seminar on…" and vague phrases like "modern trends".
- [ ] **Approval** — submit the topic form with three references; keep the signed copy.
- [ ] **Originality check** — ensure the topic is not a duplicate of a previous seminar; check the departmental register.

### 2. Literature search and appraisal

A seminar is only as good as its sources. Use primary, current, peer-reviewed material.

**Study:** Kumar, "Reviewing the literature"; [Purdue OWL "Conducting research" and IEEE reference list](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/ieee_style/ieee_overview.html).
**Exam:** No paper. Deliverable: an annotated bibliography of 10–15 sources.

- [ ] **Search strategy** — keywords, synonyms, Boolean operators; IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, Google Scholar.
- [ ] **Source hierarchy** — journal articles and standards first; conference papers; textbooks for background; avoid blogs and AI-generated text.
- [ ] **Reading method** — abstract → conclusions → figures → method; take structured notes with page numbers.
- [ ] **Critical appraisal** — method, sample, assumptions, limitations, conflicts of interest; compare findings in a table.
- [ ] **Synthesis** — group by theme, identify consensus and disagreement, state the gap or trend.
- [ ] **Citation** — IEEE numeric style, consistent; reference manager; never cite a paper you have not read.

### 3. Seminar report writing

The report is the permanent record. It must stand alone without your presentation.

**Study:** [Purdue OWL IEEE general format, tables and figures](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/ieee_style/ieee_overview.html); Beer & McMurrey, report-writing chapter.
**Exam:** No paper. Deliverable: an 8–12 page seminar report.

- [ ] **Structure** — title page, abstract, table of contents, introduction, main body (3–5 sections), conclusion, references, appendices.
- [ ] **Abstract** — 150–250 words: context, aim, method, key findings, conclusion.
- [ ] **Introduction** — background, problem, aim, scope, report organisation.
- [ ] **Main body** — logical sections with descriptive headings; each paragraph makes one point.
- [ ] **Figures and tables** — numbered, captioned, referenced; redrawn rather than screenshotted where possible.
- [ ] **Conclusion** — what the evidence shows, limitations, practical implications, future work.
- [ ] **Format** — font, spacing, margins, page numbers, binding; follow the departmental template exactly.

### 4. Oral presentation

The presentation is a performance with a technical payload. Clarity beats completeness.

**Study:** Beer & McMurrey, oral-presentation chapter; Purdue OWL "Writing in Engineering" for slide wording.
**Exam:** No paper. Deliverable: a 15-minute presentation with slides.

- [ ] **Structure** — 12–15 slides: title, outline, context, problem, method, findings (3–5 slides), discussion, conclusion, references.
- [ ] **Slide design** — one message per slide, large sans-serif font, high contrast, minimal text, labelled diagrams.
- [ ] **Timing** — rehearse to 80% of the slot; mark transition points; prepare a shorter version in case of overrun.
- [ ] **Delivery** — speak to the audience, not the screen; vary pace; pause after key points; define acronyms.
- [ ] **Handling questions** — listen, repeat, answer directly, admit uncertainty, offer to follow up.
- [ ] **Rehearsal** — at least two full run-throughs with a colleague; fix the slides that confuse them.

### 5. Question-and-answer defence

The panel tests depth, ownership and honesty. Most questions are predictable.

**Study:** no new reading — re-read your own report and prepare answers to the three obvious questions: why this topic, what is the evidence, what is the recommendation.
**Exam:** No paper. Deliverable: a mock defence in front of a peer.

- [ ] **Anticipate questions** — why this topic, why these sources, what is the engineering significance, what are the limitations.
- [ ] **Evidence file** — keep key papers, data and calculations at hand; know the numbers you quoted.
- [ ] **Answer technique** — direct answer first, then justification; do not bluff.
- [ ] **Difficult questions** — acknowledge the gap, give your best reasoning, state how you would find out.
- [ ] **Time management** — keep answers to 60–90 seconds; invite follow-up.
- [ ] **Etiquette** — stand, listen without interrupting, thank the panel, take notes for revision.

### 6. Industrial exchange report

The industrial report connects practice to theory. It is not a diary; it is an engineering analysis.

**Study:** SIWES/industrial-training guidelines (logbook, supervisor assessment, report format); departmental exchange report template.
**Exam:** No paper. Deliverable: the industrial exchange report.

- [ ] **Company profile** — name, location, products, organisation, capacity, markets; keep confidential details out.
- [ ] **Process description** — raw material → process → product; block flow diagram; identify unit operations.
- [ ] **Automation and control** — PLCs, DCS, SCADA, sensors, actuators, fieldbus; compare with MCE 511/513 theory.
- [ ] **Maintenance and safety** — maintenance strategy, PPE, permits, emergency procedures, housekeeping.
- [ ] **Observations and analysis** — problems seen, possible causes, engineering recommendations; separate fact from opinion.
- [ ] **Report format** — title page, certification, abstract, chapters, references, appendix (logbook, photographs, drawings).

### 7. Logbook, portfolio and professional conduct

The logbook is the evidence that you attended and learned. Fill it daily, not at the end.

**Study:** SIWES guidelines; COREN/NSE code of conduct (awareness only).
**Exam:** No paper. Deliverable: a signed logbook and a portfolio of evidence.

- [ ] **Daily entries** — date, department, task, observation, lesson; supervisor signature weekly.
- [ ] **Portfolio** — logbook, report drafts, presentation slides, certificates, photographs (with permission).
- [ ] **Punctuality and dress** — industrial norms; safety boots, helmet, overalls where required.
- [ ] **Confidentiality** — no proprietary data or photographs without written permission.
- [ ] **Professional ethics** — honesty, respect, safety of self and others, no plagiarism in the report.
- [ ] **Supervisor feedback** — request it early; incorporate it before final submission.

## How to use this

There is no exam paper: the "Exam" line names the **deliverable**. The course is assessed on the report, the talk and the logbook, so produce each artefact early and get feedback. Tick a box when the artefact exists and has been reviewed.

**Refresh before starting (no new textbook):** technical writing — one idea per paragraph, captions that stand alone; referencing — IEEE numbering; public speaking — timing and eye contact.

**Priority:** the literature review and the report are graded hardest; the presentation is mostly delivery. Keep the industrial logbook current — it cannot be reconstructed at the end.
